The symptoms are almost charming in their consistency. The room agreed, the doc was polished, the air still tingled with “alignment.” A week later: silence. Or worse: urgency, all nerves, no nerve center.

Everyone remembers the offsite. Fewer remember the decision. By the time the message reaches execution, it’s less telephone, more smoke signal.

Overheard in the conversion layer
“We just need to align the paradigm.”
“Let’s park this until we have bandwidth.”
“I thought we were all on the same page?”
“Can we circle back after Q3 planning?”
Most teams do not fail at strategy. They fail at translation.

Most organizations don’t die from ambition. They bleed out through ambiguity · not with a bang, but via a thousand Slack threads and one missing log. Speculative architecture. Very convincing from a distance.

There’s no word for this problem, which is part of the problem. You can’t fix what you can’t name.

The Translation Ladder

Every plan starts clean. By the time it reaches the people doing the work, it’s a four-stage degradation: decision → narration → interpretation → execution. Nobody mishears. Nobody is careless. Meaning just leaks at every handoff.

The Hidden Anti-Pattern: Humans as API

For all the digital swagger,

most organizations run on one very analog integration.

Carol in accounting.

Or Tom, still keeping the real logic in a notebook.

Documents vanish.

Slides multiply.

The only unbroken chain leads to the one person

who “remembers what we really meant.”

When humans are the only API between strategy and execution, scalability is an urban legend.

It works... until that person leaves, pivots, or gets “reallocated.” People are essential. People are not process. That’s precisely why execution becomes structurally fragile and remarkably stable in blame assignment.

Decks: Where Strategy Goes to Take a Nap

Decks are famous for two things.

1. Winning the meeting.

2. Losing everything that happens after.

They snapshot intent.

They fossilize context.

They do not, contrary to legend, translate anything.

Decks do not record trade-offs.

They do not spell out who walked away with what.

They do not stop priorities from mutating by next Tuesday.

Infrastructure fiction.

Slide edition.

  • Priorities “shift” every Monday, like civic weather.
  • No one can produce decisive meeting notes. Only confident recollections.
  • Status updates: ninety percent interpretation, ten percent hope.
  • Ownership assignments as stable as startup valuations.
  • After attrition: nobody knows what remains, but everyone agrees it was important.

Translation is not magic.

It is infrastructure.

Most places treat it like drizzle.

The gap was never strategy.

It was always the space in between.

Systems built for translation carry what gets decided.

Into what actually gets done.

They survive context shifts.

They survive calendar flips.

They survive the departure of Carol.

Name the gap.

Build the bridge.

Watch what changes.

Strategy gets the credit. Translation does the work.

Institutional Infrastructure

Scaffolding the Translation Gap

The translation gap isn’t a culture problem. It’s a tooling gap. When the load-bearing infrastructure between strategy and execution is a slide deck and Carol’s memory, of course it fractures.

I’ve spent the last several months building the system I kept wishing existed... one that carries decisions from the room where they’re made to the people who have to ship them. It’s not a SaaS product. It runs inside my advisory work, where I can tune it to what each team actually does on a Tuesday.
