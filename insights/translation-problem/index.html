You Don't Have a
Strategy Problem.

You have a translation problem. And the cost of skipping one rung is all the friction you feel.

By Luis Gilberto

Most teams don't fail at strategy. They fail at translation.

The strategy exists. The team is capable. The direction is clear. And yet somewhere between the room where the decision was made and the week execution was supposed to begin, the thread goes quiet - and no one can say exactly when it happened.

A decision gets made. No one writes it down. One team interprets it one way, another interprets it differently, a third assumes the priority has already shifted. By the time work begins, three versions of the same strategy are in motion. None of them quite right.

The thinking was there. The intent was real. Then time passed, and the sentences started.

Two weeks later
"Wait, are we still doing that?"
"I thought that was just a suggestion."
"We decided that?"
"Why are we building this again?"

Work continues. But not in a line. And we reach for the familiar diagnosis: better alignment. Clearer communication. A stronger strategy.

The Root Issue

Those symptoms point to something quieter and more common: the space between thinking and doing is wildly underscaffolded.

Strategy Lives Up High. Execution Lives Down Low.

Strategy is altitude: direction, intent, possibility. Execution is ground truth: precision, prioritization, trade-offs, ownership, timing.

Failure doesn't usually happen on either end. It happens in the conversion layer between them - the space where intent becomes interpretation, and interpretation becomes action.

A leadership team aligns on a product direction during a two-day offsite. The PM leaves with one set of priorities. The engineers leave with another. Neither is wrong. They simply filled in the gaps with different assumptions, and no one noticed until the rework started six weeks later.

The reason this pattern repeats regardless of industry, team size, or leadership quality isn't a people problem. It's a structural one. Every time strategy passes through hands, something is lost or reinterpreted. Not out of carelessness. Out of absence - the absence of a system designed to carry meaning forward.

The Hidden Anti-Pattern: Humans Become the API

Here's what's happening in most organizations. Slides live in shared folders no one can find. Roadmaps live in tools. Tickets live in systems. But meaning lives in people.

So humans become the glue. They become the translator layer: the person who remembers what was really meant, the person who catches what got dropped, the person who reframes the plan every time new context arrives, the person who holds the thread so everything doesn't unravel.

Someone remembers a decision that was never documented. Someone else interprets a slide differently. A third assumes alignment because no one said otherwise. Nothing is technically wrong, but everything begins to drift.

We've made humans the API between
strategy and execution.
It works until it doesn't.

Until someone forgets. Until something changes. Until someone leaves. Until someone's on vacation. Until you scale.

Humans are essential. But they're not a scalable operating system. That's why things feel fragile. Rework is constant. Momentum is hard to maintain. Execution feels high-effort even when the team is strong.

The Diagnosis

Capability isn't missing. Structure is.

The Translation Ladder

Translation is not making it simpler. It's making it actionable. It's turning a high-level idea into a low-level reality without losing the thread. That requires moving through a specific sequence: direction to decisions, decisions to priorities, priorities to trade-offs, trade-offs to ownership, ownership to measurable progress.

Most teams skip this sequence entirely. They compress it. Assume it's happening through meetings and conversations. But it's happening inside people's heads - unevenly, inconsistently, and invisibly. Each rung that gets skipped becomes a gap. Each gap becomes a source of friction that no one can name.

The Translation Ladder: A scaffold you can actually use
1
Direction → Decisions

What are we deciding explicitly because of this strategy?

2
Decisions → Priorities

What moves to the top, and what drops down? Without explicit ranking, everything is the priority - which means nothing is.

3
Priorities → Trade-offs

What are we not doing? What are we de-scoping? What are we delaying? Trade-offs are where strategy becomes real. Skipping this step is how you end up doing everything poorly.

4
Trade-offs → Ownership & Next Actions

Who owns what? What's the very next step? What does "done" mean?

5
Actions → Measures & Cadence

How will we know if it's working, and how often will we review it?

This ladder looks obvious. It isn't. Most of the pain inside execution is the accumulated cost of skipping one rung - and not knowing which one.

Why Decks Are Where Good Strategy Goes to Nap

Strategy decks aren't useless. They're just incomplete. A deck captures direction. But direction isn't the problem.

Decks freeze thinking in time. They preserve the logic of the moment the slide was written - not the living complexity of what execution will actually demand. They create the sensation of alignment: everyone nods, the meeting ends, the deck gets filed. But a nod is not a decision. A filed deck is not a system. And shared presence in a room is not the same as shared understanding of what comes next.

Decks don't encode trade-offs. They don't record what was decided against. They don't show who owns what when priorities collide. And they don't survive contact with execution - where context shifts, timelines compress, and the clean logic of the slide meets the friction of actual people doing actual work.

None of this is a failure of the people who built the deck. It's a failure of the format.

What's Missing

The decisions log. The trade-off record. The "what must be true" list. The ownership map. The review cadence. Without those, strategy becomes a moment in time: a phase, a deliverable, a "hit send."

Execution isn't that. Execution is a living system. And until we build systems that connect thinking to doing, friction is guaranteed.

Strategy becomes a moment in time.
Execution is a living system.
We confuse the two constantly.

The problem isn't that we don't know how to think.
It's that we haven't built systems designed to carry thinking forward.

So the work isn't more strategy.
It's better translation.

I've been building something that lives in that space.

Not a framework.
Not a deck.
Not another layer of interpretation.

Something that actually processes strategy and connects it to execution - structurally, not aspirationally.

Because teams don't have a strategy problem. They have a translation problem. And translation, it turns out, is an infrastructure problem.

It's not ready to be named yet.

But it's coming.

And when it does, the conversation about strategy and execution will need a new vocabulary.
