The meeting had gone well.

Everyone agreed on the priorities. The brief was tight. The timeline made sense.

And as people filed out, there was that particular feeling you get when a room has actually aligned — not performed alignment, but reached it.

I drove home thinking we were ready.

Three weeks later
Not in the work.
Not in how the team was moving.
Not in what we were building.

The thinking had simply disappeared.

I assumed it was a one-time failure — a communication breakdown we could fix with a better follow-up email, a cleaner brief, a more disciplined kickoff. We'd been close to aligned. We just needed to close the gap.

So we did.

And then the same thing happened again.

The First Time I Really Noticed

It was a product launch — the kind with enough complexity that every team had a legitimate claim on the strategy.

In the room
Marketing.
Product.
Engineering.
Sales.

Each group had been in the room. Each group had heard the same conversation. Each group left with a different understanding of what we'd decided.

By the time execution began, we weren't executing one strategy.

We were executing four interpretations of one strategy.

Nobody was wrong. Everyone was working from what they remembered. But memory is lossy. And strategy communicated only through conversation starts degrading the moment the meeting ends.

What Struck Me

Not the confusion — confusion is normal in complex work. What struck me was how quickly the original thinking became inaccessible. Not just to the broader team. To the people who had been in the room.

Then It Happened Again

Different product. Different team. Different launch.

Same collapse.

This time, the strategy had been documented — a proper deck, tight narrative, clearly articulated trade-offs. We'd spent real time on it. I remember being proud of it.

Six weeks into execution, the brief was two versions old and nobody could tell me which one was current.

The trade-offs we'd made in week one had been quietly relitigated in week three when a stakeholder asked a question and nobody could point to the decision that answered it. So the team made a new decision. And then another.

Each one reasonable in isolation

Each one slowly unwinding the original logic. By the time we shipped, we had built something technically consistent with the strategy but spiritually distant from it. The words matched. The intent didn't.

I started paying closer attention.

What I Kept Ruling Out

For a while, I blamed communication.

If we just explained the strategy more clearly, it would hold. So teams got better presentations, more thorough briefs, more frequent alignment meetings.

The thinking still dissolved.

Then I blamed commitment.

Maybe people weren't bought in. Maybe the strategy hadn't been co-created with enough of the right voices. So we ran more inclusive planning processes, brought more stakeholders in earlier.

The thinking still dissolved.

Then I wondered if it was a leadership problem.

Maybe execution teams needed stronger direction, more active management of strategic intent. So we built in more check-ins, more reviews, more opportunities to course-correct.

The thinking still dissolved.

Three diagnoses. Same result.
1
Communication problem?

Better presentations, cleaner briefs, more alignment meetings. The thinking still dissolved.

2
Commitment problem?

More inclusive planning, earlier stakeholder involvement, co-creation at every level. The thinking still dissolved.

3
Leadership problem?

Stronger direction, more check-ins, more active management of strategic intent. The thinking still dissolved.

Each diagnosis led to a reasonable intervention. Each intervention helped at the margins.

None of them solved the underlying problem.

The Shift

At some point I stopped asking what we were doing wrong and started asking what was structurally missing.

The Pattern

Over the next several years, I kept a loose inventory of moments like these.

Not formally — just a running awareness I carried into every launch, every campaign, every cross-functional initiative.

The details changed. The pattern didn't.

A campaign would open with sharp positioning and close with diluted messaging.

A product launch would begin with clear prioritization and arrive at market with features that contradicted the original trade-offs.

A cross-team initiative would align in the planning phase and fragment the moment execution introduced pressure.

What I kept noticing was not that strategy was bad.
The breakdown was happening somewhere else.

In most cases, the strategic thinking was genuinely good. The people were talented. The intentions were serious. The work started from a real place.

The breakdown was happening somewhere between the thinking and the doing.

Once you see the gap as structural, you can't unsee it.

The Structural Realization

Here is what I eventually came to understand.

Strategy is not a document. It is not a meeting.

Strategy is a body of thinking — decisions made, trade-offs accepted, options deliberately set aside. It has weight and texture. It has a logic that connects early choices to later ones.

And organizations have almost no infrastructure for preserving that thinking.

What exists
Tools for managing tasks.
Tools for tracking timelines.
Tools for communicating status.

We have almost nothing designed to hold strategic intent as it moves through execution. Nothing that keeps the original decisions visible. Nothing that makes trade-offs legible to people who weren't in the room. Nothing that lets a team in week six understand why something was decided in week one.

So the thinking dissolves.

Not because people are careless.

Because there is nothing to hold it.

This was the moment I stopped treating strategy as a communication challenge
and started seeing it as an engineering one.

And engineering problems don't get solved by trying harder.

They get solved by building infrastructure.

The Question

I didn't know what to build yet.

That would come later, through a series of experiments that started small and got more deliberate over time.

But I knew the question. And once I had it, I couldn't set it down.

That realization changed the question I was asking.

What would it take for strategy
to survive contact with execution?

Not survive in theory. Not survive in the deck that gets archived the week after launch.

Survive in the actual work. In the decisions made at 11 a.m. on a Tuesday when nobody is looking at the brief. In the trade-offs that get relitigated under pressure. In the moments when a team has to choose between two reasonable options and needs to know which one the strategy actually supports.

The Real Question

What would it take for the thinking to travel? That question changed how I approached my work. It changed what I noticed. It changed what I started building — slowly, informally — in the spaces between official process.

What Came Next

I want to be careful here.

This is not the part where I tell you I solved it.

I didn't solve it. Not all at once, and not alone.

What happened was more gradual than that. The question kept surfacing in different contexts, and each time it did, I tried something.

Small experiments
A different way of documenting decisions.
A different structure for linking strategy to execution milestones.
A different format for making trade-offs visible to people who hadn't been in the room.

Some of it worked. Some of it didn't.

All of it taught me something about the shape of the problem.

And slowly, across enough iterations, something started to take form. Not as a product. Not yet as a system. But as a point of view — a belief that the gap between strategy and execution wasn't inevitable. That it was an engineering problem. And that it could be addressed with the right infrastructure.

I didn't know yet what I was going to build.

I wasn't thinking about systems or tools or frameworks.

I was just sitting with a question that wouldn't leave me alone.

And questions like that tend to do something interesting.

If you follow them long enough, they stop being questions.

They start becoming infrastructure.

Slowly. Then all at once.
