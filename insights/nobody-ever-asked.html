Nobody Ever Asked

Most people are carrying abilities that have never been requested. That is a different problem than not having them.

By Luis Gilberto Sánchez  ·  3 minute read

I left Microsoft after thirteen years, and the part I remember most clearly is not the last day. It is the first ordinary Tuesday after.

I woke at the same hour out of habit, made coffee, sat down, and opened a calendar with nothing in it. I refreshed it, the way you refresh a page that has failed to load. It loaded fine. There was simply nothing there.

I had assumed the strange feeling would be about status, or income, or belonging. It was none of those. It was quieter than that, and harder to name. For thirteen years something had been asking me for things. Now nothing was asking.

That is what a large organization actually does for you, and it is almost never described this way. It sends you a steady supply of requests. Ship this. Reframe that. Get the partner to yes. Explain a complicated product to eleven million people by March.

The requests are specific, they arrive on a schedule, and they are addressed to whichever part of you the org chart knows about. Mine knew about the marketer. It made requests of the marketer for thirteen years, and the marketer got very good.

It never once asked me to design an interface. It never asked me to build software, name a methodology, or write a sentence in my own voice. Not because anyone doubted I could. Because that was not the role, and roles are how large organizations decide what to ask for.

Nothing about that night was new capability.

Some months later I was up past one in the morning adjusting the space between two letters on a page almost nobody had seen. I nudged the tracking, refreshed, leaned back, nudged it again. At some point I noticed I had been at it for over an hour and that I was completely, unreasonably happy.

I had not acquired a skill that week. The letterforms had been sitting there my whole career. What was new was that the request had come from me.

I think this is far more common than we let on. Most people are carrying abilities that no environment has ever put in a request for.

Someone has remarkable visual instincts and never learned the software. Someone thinks structurally and was never invited to build a system. Someone spent fifteen years translating complexity for other people without once noticing that translation is itself a skill, and a portable one.

They do not experience this as untapped potential. They experience it as plain fact. I am not a designer. I am not technical. I am not a writer. Those sentences feel like self-knowledge. Usually they are just an accurate description of what has been asked.

This is the part of the AI conversation I find genuinely interesting, and it is not the part anyone argues about on the internet. These tools did not make me more capable. They lowered the cost of asking myself for something. The distance between a half-formed idea and a thing you can actually look at got short enough that you can make the request before your own doubt has time to answer it.

Used carelessly, they produce astonishing quantities of mediocrity. Used deliberately, they let a person work past the edge of their formal training long enough to find out what is actually in there.

The request

Left blank, because nobody is going to send this one.

No manager, market, or algorithm is going to notice the ability you have never demonstrated and politely ask you for it.

That is not the tragedy. That is the job.

This is the short version of an argument I made at length in What I Built After Microsoft, a 13 minute read about what happened when I started making the requests myself.
