A Personal Essay

What I Built After Microsoft

I thought I was building a website. It turned into a practice, a product, and a late education in what I am actually for.

By Luis Gilberto Sánchez  ·  Plates by LG Studio
13 minute read  ·  3,100 words · 7 plates
Insights · What I Built After Microsoft1

It was past one in the morning and I was adjusting the space between two letters.

Not a paragraph. Not a headline. Two letters, in a single word, on a page that almost nobody had seen. I nudged the tracking, refreshed, leaned back, nudged it again. At some point I noticed I had been doing this for well over an hour and that I was completely, unreasonably happy.

That was the first signal, and I misread it completely. I thought I was making a place to put my work. I was building the room the next part of my career would happen in.

The Institution

What thirteen years actually gave me

I spent more than thirteen years at Microsoft, across consumer marketing, retail, Microsoft 365, Edge, and eventually Copilot. It taught me how ideas actually move through the world. I learned how to translate complicated technology into something a person could understand, care about, and use. I learned how global campaigns get built, how partnerships create scale, how an executive decision travels through an organization and arrives, barely recognizable, at the person who has to execute it.

None of that was wasted. The version of this story where the corporation is the villain is both boring and false.

The tension was never Microsoft. It was structural. Inside a company of that scale, your ability is expressed through a role, and the environment around that role already exists. There are teams, budgets, systems, precedents, and boundaries. You contribute your part, sometimes a large one, but you are rarely responsible for imagining the whole environment in which an idea will live.

Then I left, and the environment disappeared.

I refreshed it, the way you refresh a page that has failed to load. It loaded fine. There was simply nothing there.

I remember the first Tuesday. Not the last day, not the goodbye messages, the first ordinary Tuesday after. I woke up at the same hour out of habit, made coffee, sat down, and opened a calendar with nothing in it. I refreshed it, the way you refresh a page that has failed to load. It loaded fine. There was simply nothing there. No campaign milestones. No reviews. No roadmap telling me what week of what quarter this was supposed to be.

I had been so busy for so long that I had forgotten what my own attention felt like when nothing was pulling on it.

At first that space was unsettling. It took a few weeks before it became delicious.

The First Page

The website was the inciting incident

I started with a website, which sounds comically modest given what it became. But I was not trying to launch a company or invent a professional category. I wanted somewhere that belonged to me. A place to collect the work, look at it honestly, and decide how I wanted to present myself after a defining chapter.

What I did not expect was how quickly the building would turn into an interrogation.

Every page forced a decision about what I actually believed. What do I know how to do? What is the common thread across roles that looked unrelated on a résumé? What kind of work do I want to make next? And underneath all of it, the real question: what could I build now that no job description was setting the edges?

I have never been good at reflection in the abstract. I answered those questions by making things and noticing what I chose.

That night with the letter spacing was the moment the website stopped being a portfolio. A portfolio is a container. You fill it and you are done. This was behaving differently. I cared about the architecture, the typography, the transitions, the hierarchy, how it felt to move through the pages. I wanted the experience to feel considered, because consideration was the thing I was trying to communicate, and you cannot claim it in a paragraph while contradicting it in a layout.

The medium had become the argument. Once I saw that, I could not unsee it.
The Voice

Writing my way back to myself

As the site developed, I built an editorial channel and called it Insights.

The plan was modest: write about my experience, particularly my relationship with technology and what I had watched change from inside the industry. The first pieces were reflections. They turned useful faster than I expected, because writing forced me to name ideas I had been running on instinct for a decade.

I published the first few and waited to feel exposed. I did, briefly. What followed surprised me more.

I reread a paragraph I had written about how people mistake activity for direction, and I did not recognize it as marketing. It did not sound like a deck. It did not sound like anything I had produced under a logo. It sounded like the way I actually talk when I am being useful to someone at a table. I had spent years writing in institutional voice so fluently that I had stopped noticing I was doing it, and here was this other register that had been sitting underneath the whole time.

That was the moment I understood what Insights was for. I had assumed it was a way to be read. It turned out to be the only way I could hear myself.

I wrote about technology, but not from the usual positions of breathless enthusiasm or impending doom. I was interested in what technology reveals about us. I had spent years helping people understand new products. Now I wanted to understand what those products were doing to our sense of possibility, identity, work, and self-direction.

AI entered the writing naturally, but the subject was never only AI. What held my attention was a quieter possibility: that technology could help a person recognize capacities in themselves that had gone dormant.

Most people carry abilities they have never had the time or the permission to use. Someone has remarkable visual instincts and never learned the software. Someone thinks structurally and was never invited to build a system. Someone spent a career translating complexity without ever noticing that translation itself is a skill, and a portable one.

Used carelessly, these tools flatten ideas and produce astonishing quantities of mediocrity. Used deliberately, they let a person work beyond the limits of their formal training. They let you test an idea before fear has time to kill it.

AI did not give me a new identity. It helped me use abilities my previous environment had never required me to use together.

That distinction became load-bearing for everything after. The machine could accelerate the work. It could not decide what deserved to exist, and it could not supply taste, or judgment, or the memory of having been wrong before. Human direction sits above machine execution in everything I build, which is less a principle I announce than a maintenance obligation I keep.

The Architecture

One page led to a system

None of this came from a business plan. Each piece exists because it answered a question the piece before it had raised.

Insights was where the thinking happened. The thinking kept producing frameworks, and the frameworks needed somewhere to live and be reused. Those frameworks kept circling the same underlying question, which is how the diagnostic method that became StrategyIQ began. StrategyIQ then had to prove itself on real work, which is what Studio does: campaigns, products, identities, experiences. And all of it needed an operating environment where the work, the decisions, and the relationships could actually live, which became the Portal.

Thinking, system, diagnosis, execution.

That description is tidier than the experience was. In reality I was learning software development while refining a consulting methodology, designing interfaces while writing strategic frameworks, building client systems and AI workflows and decision records, and regularly wondering whether any of it belonged together.

Then I would find that it did. The same instinct was underneath all of it: take something complex, find the signal inside it, give it structure, and turn that structure into work people can use.

For years I had thought of myself as a marketer. Building the ecosystem showed me that marketing was one expression of a wider ability. I had not outgrown that background. I was finally seeing the whole of it.

The Shape

The moment the framework became a product

StrategyIQ started as a way of reading complicated situations, mostly for my own use.

I had noticed how often someone arrives with a problem that is not the problem. A brand believes it needs a campaign when what it lacks is a proposition. A founder believes they need more content when what they lack is a path from attention to action. A team believes it has an execution problem when decisions are being made without any shared sense of priority.

Activity is a very effective disguise for uncertainty.

The moment it stopped being a framework was specific and unglamorous. I was on a call, listening to someone describe a situation, and about four minutes in I realized I already knew the shape. Not the answer. The shape. I had seen this exact pattern twice before in unrelated industries, and I could feel myself reaching for something structured rather than for an opinion.

Not the answer. The shape.

I hung up and started naming the recurring shapes. If they repeated, they could be described. If they could be described, they could be tested, and this was no longer intuition I was renting out by the hour. It was a method that could exist outside my own head.

That premise became a structured methodology, then a diagnostic engine, then software. StrategyIQ takes messy human input and organizes it into patterns, constraints, priorities, and next moves.

I deliberately kept a human gate. The system can surface a likely read. A person still has to understand what it means in context and accept responsibility for the recommendation. That is why I distinguish between a First Read and a Second Read. The First Read is an immediate structured interpretation. The Second Read goes closer, and brings experience, context, and the kind of judgment pattern matching cannot get to on its own.

Later I built StrategyIQ Light for individuals and small teams who need the read without the full engagement. Same underlying intelligence, lower volume, gentler on the person sitting in front of it.

It also kept me honest. Consultants are rewarded for sounding certain quickly. This required me to show how I reached a conclusion, and to leave a visible path between the information received, the pattern identified, the priority chosen, and the action recommended. The software made the practice more rigorous than it had been when it lived only in my head.

I should say that this sequence reads much cleaner on the page than it felt at the time. There were stretches with no deadline, no external permission, and no evidence that any of it would matter to anyone but me. There were days when I could not tell whether I was constructing a practice or simply becoming exceptionally good at building things nobody had asked for yet.

The Proof

Someone else's work was the real test

An ecosystem that only serves its author is a very elaborate mirror. The test was whether any of it could hold something outside itself.

Criar Sin Culpas was already a trusted parenting practice with a substantial audience, more than fifty workshops, and a real bond with the families it served. Its founder, Nari Fateha, had built something people needed. But the knowledge, products, content, and support did not yet operate as one coherent system.

The work required far more than a refreshed website. We had to find the structure already living inside years of expertise, understand the journey parents were actually on, usually in the worst hour of a hard week, and turn a shelf of individual workshops into a curriculum, a bilingual platform, and an operating practice that could hold all of it.

The turn came on April 8, and it was not the one I expected. I ran the discovery material through StrategyIQ assuming the problem was that good knowledge was disorganized. The read said otherwise. Parents were not arriving to browse. They were arriving activated, scanning, often one-handed, looking for a single useful sentence at the worst hour of a hard week. That is not an information problem. It is a support problem, and support has to be designed for the moment it is needed. The brief changed that afternoon.

A week later, a second pattern surfaced, and it belonged to her. Working through years of workshops and teaching material, I kept meeting the same progression on topics that had nothing to do with each other: accept the emotion, stabilize the body, restore connection, then teach the limit. On April 15 I wrote those four words down and sat with them, because they had not come from me. They had been running quietly underneath everything she taught, never named and never gathered in one place. The first discovery told us what problem to solve. This one gave the answer its underlying architecture.

The work is to listen closely enough to find the structure that is present but not yet visible.

That is the distinction I care about most. I am not interested in imposing a generic operating model on someone else's expertise. The work is to listen closely enough to find the structure that is present but not yet visible, and then use technology to hold it, extend it, and activate it.

A collection of workshops became a curriculum. The curriculum became a system. The system became something she owned. From there it grew into brand strategy, bilingual content, protocols for specific parenting situations, product pathways, and the infrastructure to keep supporting families after the workshop ends.

That was the moment the experiment became a practice. I was no longer only building my own ecosystem. I could help someone else find the system inside her own work and give it somewhere to live.

The Recognition

Becoming the kind of person who builds

Someone asked me recently what I had been doing since Microsoft. It was a polite question, the kind that expects a sentence.

I started answering and watched their expression change as the sentence did not end. Websites, essays, a diagnostic engine, software products, client systems, strategic briefs, bilingual platforms, operating models, visual identities, case studies. Somewhere in the middle of it I heard myself and stopped, because I understood something I had not put into words yet.

From the outside, this period could look like a pause between jobs. From the inside it has been the most productive chapter of my working life.

What changed most was not the software or the practice. I stopped being afraid of making things that did not yet have permission to exist.

Inside a large organization, legitimacy is issued by the institution.

Inside a large organization, legitimacy is issued by the institution. A project has a budget, a sponsor, a launch date, a team. Outside that structure you have to create legitimacy differently, and it usually begins with nothing more impressive than deciding that an idea deserves a page.

Then you publish the page. You see what is missing. You build the next piece and test it on a real person, who tells you where the thinking fails. Eventually you notice that the thing you thought was a website has become a framework, and the framework has become a product, and the product has become a practice that helped someone else build something she could not have built alone.

Volume was never the point. All of it came from learning to work differently. AI gave me reach. Everything that decided where to point it came from somewhere else.

I left Microsoft without a plan. I left with thirteen years of accumulated experience and a suspicion that it could add up to something more personal than a résumé.

The website gave me somewhere to begin. Everything since has been the same move repeated at a larger scale.

I still think of this as discovery rather than reinvention. Reinvention suggests becoming someone new, and that is not what happened. The strategist, the storyteller, the designer, the builder were all already in there. Some had been used hard for years. Others had never once been asked.

Technology did not create those parts of me.

It gave me a way to wake them up.
